ABSTRACT  
Robotic odor/gas source localization is a widely studied field, but most of the existing works are about rule-based 
algorithms. In this paper, the Deep Q-Network algorithm is applied to solve the odor source localization problem. 
An odor hits distribution model is proposed to model the odor concentration distribution in indoor environments, 
taking the dispersion by airflow, the odor molecular random walk, and the obstacles into account. The Deep Q- 
Network takes the stacked historic measurement data as the input and outputs the expected cumulative future 
reward of actions of the robots. The network is trained through 35,000 repeated episodes of random odor source 
localization tasks. The Deep Q-Network method is evaluated under four different environment settings in a 
simplified simulator and compared with two widely used odor source localization algorithms. The evaluation 
results demonstrate the advantages of the proposed algorithm. The algorithm is also validated in more complex 
indoor environments.   
1. Introduction 
Robotic odor/gas source localization (OSL/GSL) is a cross-discipline 
involved with ethology [1 – 4] , computational fluid dynamics [5,6] , 
sensing technologies [7,8] , probability theory [9 – 12] , path planning 
algorithms [13,14] and mobile robotics [15,16] . OSL in an indoor 
environment is an important but challenging task, especially for custom 
security and disaster rescue. Drugs and bombs release chemical plumes, 
which cannot be easily smelled by humans. Police dogs are often 
employed in these tasks because of their sensitive noses. However, some 
dangerous situations, such as tracking poisonous gas leakage, would 
harm the safety of these searching animals and their human handlers. 
Robotic solutions are a good substitute for living searching animals. 
They do not need much training from expert human handlers and can be 
produced in a large amount. They don ’ t need rest in long-time tasks, 
either. Compared with static sensor network solutions [17,18] , robotic 
solutions require less sensor nodes and can be adjustable to various 
environments. 
Researches on robotic OSL started from the early 1900s. Various OSL 
algorithms have been proposed, which can be roughly divided into four 
categories [19,16] : gradient climbing algorithms, bio-inspired algo -
rithms, probabilistic based algorithms, and multi-robot cooperation 
algorithms. 
Works on gradient climbing algorithms are early works in the OSL 
field [20,21] . The robots climb the odor concentration gradient to locate 
the odor source, where the concentration is usually the highest. How -
ever, since the odor plumes are propagated by airflow, which is often 
turbulent and may have eddies, the odor concentration gradient is not as 
smooth as researchers expected. The robots tend to spiral in the 
searching area and it will take a long time for them to locate the odor 
source [16] . Bio-inspired algorithms [1 – 4,22,23] are designed to pro -
gram the robots to mimic the living creature ’ s behavior when searching 
for food and mates. These algorithms are easy to implement and 
computationally cheap, but their searching time is not short, either [9] . 
Probabilistic based algorithms [9 – 12] model the location of the odor 
source as a probability distribution and update the location estimation 
through iterative observations at different positions in the searching 
area. These algorithms are reported to outperform some gradient 
climbing and bio-inspired algorithms, but they are hard to implement 
and of high computational cost [9] . Multi-robot cooperation algorithms

[24 – 26] encourage the robot to share knowledge about history obser -

Most of the above algorithms are rule-based algorithms. The robot 
will take movements according to its observations of odor concentration 
and airflow direction, following a preset rule. These rules (e.g. fixed 
odor concentration baseline, fixed odor plume distribution model) are 
often built for a specific application scenario. Thus the algorithms are of 
relatively poor robustness for various environmental conditions [27] . 
Some fuzzy logic rule-based control algorithms [4,28 – 30] are proposed 
to promote the robustness, but it is complex to set dedicated fuzzy rules 
and membership functions. 
Machine learning algorithms have been widely studied in the last 
decades. Reinforcement learning (RL) [31] is a branch of machine 
learning algorithms that let the agents learn an optimal action policy to 
maximize the expected cumulative future reward. The action policy is 
learned from a large number of repeated attempts under different 
environment settings, so it is expected to be robust and can solve the 
above issues of the rule-based algorithm. 
RL have been applied in radiation source localization [32] , sound 
source localization [33,34] , and walking-aid robot [35] . For OSL, [36] 
applied a reinforcement learning-based multi-robot algorithm to opti -
mize OSL performance across group size. [37] combined RL with 
partially observable Markov decision process for autonomous under -
water vehicles ’ plume tracing undersea. But these two works only 
studied OSL in searching areas without obstacles. RL based OSL in the 
area with obstacles has not been reported in the literature. 
Based on this consideration, a Deep Q-Network (DQN) based OSL 
algorithm is proposed in this paper. The algorithm is designed to guide 
the robot to locate the odor source, which is of unknown releasing rate, 
with minimum movement steps, and avoid obstacles in the searching 
area. The performance of the proposed algorithm is evaluated under 
different environment settings (with/ without obstacles, upwind/ 
downwind starting positions of the robot, various source release rates, in 
multiple rooms). The proposed algorithm is compared with a gradient 
climbing algorithm in all settings, and a bio-inspired algorithm in two 
settings where the robot starts from downwind positions. 
Our main contributions are as follows:  
(1) An odor hits distribution model has been proposed. The odor 
plumes ’ dispersion through airflow, the odor molecular random 
walk, and the existence of obstacles are all considered.  
(2) A DQN based OSL algorithm with experience replay is designed. 
A convolutional neural network is built for training the action- 
value function. The historic measurement data matrices are 
stacked to form the input of the neural network. 
(3) The proposed OSL method is tested in various indoor environ -
ments and compared with other two widely used methods. 
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The introduction of the 
OSL task and the odor hits distribution model are presented in Section 2 . 
The proposed DQN based OSL algorithm and its training details are in 
Section 3 . Case study for different environmental conditions in the 
simplified simulator and the comparison between algorithms are pre -
sented in Section 4 . The validation of the DQN based algorithm in more 
complex and realistic environments simulated by GADEN [5] is pre -
sented in Section 5 . 
2. The odor source localization task 
The OSL task addressed in this paper is to find a single odor source in 
an enclosed 10 m × 10 m 2D area, with obstacles inside, as efficiently as 
possible. The area is modeled as a grid. The size of each cell is 1 m × 1 m. 
The agent that is going to complete the task is called the olfactory robot. 
Since the robot can build the map of the environment with SLAM 
techniques, and SLAM is not among our main contribution of the paper, 
in this work, the olfactory robot is assumed to have the prior knowledge 
of the size of the area, the location of the obstacles and itself. It can also 
measure the odor concentration and the airflow direction at its position. 
Conventionally, the OSL task can be divided into three subtasks:  
• Plume finding - searching in the environment to find odor plumes  
• Plume tracking - searching towards the direction the odor plumes 
come  
• Source declaration - arriving at the immediate vicinity of the odor 
source and determining its location 
Due to the sophisticated dispersion of the odor plume, the olfactory 
robot often has to address the first two subtasks alternately. So these two 
subtasks are regarded as a whole in this paper. Normally, the source 
declaration subtask can be achieved by recognition of odor acquisition 
characteristics or other sensing modalities, such as vision. Therefore, in 
this paper, it is assumed that the robot can locate the odor source when it 
arrives within 1 m from the source. Similar assumptions are also made in 
previous OSL papers [38 – 40] . 
Odor concentration is measured in a discrete number of hits , a unit 
which was also used in [41,9] . According to the physical nature of the 
odor plume propagation, the odor concentration at a certain position 
may be the outcome of two processes: one is the dispersion by the 
airflow, the other one is the random walk of odor molecules. Inspired by 
[9] , we proposed the following equations to model the hits measured by 
the odor sensor:

3. DQN based OSL algorithm 
In this work, DQN [42] from reinforcement learning is applied to 
train the olfactory robot to locate odor sources. In this section, we 
discuss the interaction between the olfactory robot and the environ -
ment, present our proposed DQN based OSL algorithm, and show the 
training details of the DQN. 
3.1. The olfactory robot-environment interaction

plumes, the airflow, and the obstacles, are called the environment. As 
shown in Fig. 2 , the movements of the olfactory robot in an OSL task can

odor hits the olfactory robot measures at its location and the knowledge

4. Case study and performance evaluation in the simplified 
simulator 
In this section, we study the performance of the proposed DQN based 
OSL algorithm in different cases and compare it with a widely studied 
gradient climbing (GC) algorithm and an anemotaxis bio-inspired al -
gorithm, Surge-Cast (SC). In the GC algorithm, the robot always goes to 
the direction where the odor concentration increases fastest. In the SC 
algorithm, the robot goes upwind when it is in the plume, and conducts 
the crosswind search when it goes out of the plume. The details about GC 
and SC are presented in Appendix B and C . 
4.1. Environment settings 
The performance of the proposed algorithm is evaluated under four 
different map settings: The robot starts from the downwind/ upwind 
direction of the odor source and there is/ is not an obstacle in the 
searching area. The four maps are illustrated in Fig. 6 . 
Under each map setting, we set the release rate parameter of the odor 
source to be 20 , 40 , 60 , 80 , 100. For each combination of the map setting 
and the release rate, we conducted 200 repeated runs with the proposed 
DQN based algorithm, GC and SC, respectively to compare the perfor -
mance difference between them. Since the original form of GC and SC do 
not include obstacle avoidance, we programmed the robot to actively

algorithm: (1) success rate, and (2) distance overhead. 
• Success rate - this metric for evaluating the feasibility of the algo -
rithms. It is defined as the number of successful runs, in which the 
robot reaches within 1 m from the odor source, per total number of 
runs (200 in this work). 
• Distance overhead - this metric reflects the efficiency of the algo -
rithms. It is defined as the traveled distance of the olfactory robot 
when reaching the odor source per shortest distance. Since the

5.2. Train and test the algorithm in the high-fidelity simulator 
In this section, the effect of using various airflow patterns during 
training is studies. Based on the previous trained network, 1500 addi -
tional training episodes are added, with the robot starting from random 
positions in the top two rooms of the multiple room scenario. The added 
training episodes bring in various patterns and make fuller use of the 
“ mean measured airflow direction matrix ” . 
After the additional training, we tested the DQN algorithm in 3 
scenarios: the multiple room scenario and the central obstacle room 
scenario shown in Fig. 12 , plus a rotated central obstacle room scenario 
(shown in Fig. 14 ). The rotated central obstacle room scenario was built 
by rotating the original map clockwise for 90 degrees, together with the 
odor concentration distribution and the airflow distribution. 200 
repeated runs were conducted with the robot starting from random 
positions in the three scenarios. The success rate and the distance 
overhead are shown in Fig. 15 . 
Compared with the original DQN algorithm (the results are shown in 
Fig. 13 ), the retrained DQN algorithm achieved much lower distance 
overhead in the multiple rooms scenario. However, the success rate 
declined from 79.5% to 77.5%. It has to be noted that the robot was set 
to start from the top two rooms in the retraining process conducted in 
the multi-room scenario, so the retrained network may be more capable 
for situations where the airflow changes a lot, but may sacrifice per -
formance in the steady airflow scenarios. The performance evaluation 
was conducted in the whole area in the multi-room scenario. This can 
explain why the distance overhead of the retrained DQN is much lower 
than that in Fig. 13 - the robot goes alone a more straightforward path 
when it starts from the top rooms, and why the overall success rate is a 
bit lower - the success rate may decline a bit when the robot starts from 
the bottom room. This unobvious difference on the success rate may also 
due to the random tests and it does not mean a substantial performance 
degrade. In the central obstacle scenario, the success rate and the dis -
tance overhead of the retrained DQN are both better than the original 
DQN shown in Fig. 13 . The retrained DQN algorithm can also achieve 
88.5% success rate in the rotated scenario, which has never appeared in 
the training process. In contrast, when the original DQN algorithm was 
tested in the rotated scenario, it can only achieve 32.5% success rate, 
which is much lower than that of the retrained algorithm. It can be 
inferred that after training with various airflow patterns, the robot got 
more guidance from the “ mean measured airflow direction matrix ” , thus 
the performance of the algorithm was promoted. More elaborate 
training process with various maps and airflow patterns may improve 
the algorithm further. 
6. Discussion 
From the previous tests in various environments, it is clear that the 
proposed algorithm can learn from a set of given training environments, 
and the ability to later apply a learnt optimal action policy in similar 
environments in the simplified simulator. To some extent, the proposed 
method is also able to generalize for environments with different airflow 
patterns or obstacles with different shapes (e.g. multi-room scenarios in 
GADEN). In very different environments, the proposed algorithm can 
achieve an acceptable success rate, but the distance overhead increases 
drastically. Because very different states are fed into the CNN, the pre -
viously trained algorithm cannot always guide the robot to the right 
direction. Additional training processes are required to solve this issue. 
Also, the proposed algorithm is designed and trained for 10 m × 10 m 
environments, when applied in environments with different dimensions, 
a scaling process is required. Besides, the proposed algorithm relies on 
knowledge of the map of the scenario and the position of the robot. 
We have also tested the algorithm in the multiple room scenario with 
the odor source located inside top left room and the robot starting from 
the top right room. The results turned out to be that the robot could 
hardly find the odor source and it moved along heavily back-and-forth 
trajectories. This result was due to the fact that the hits distribution in 
the GADEN simulator was different from what we built to train the

gorithm. The proposed method is expected to be a benchmark for the 
following works in this aspect. 
7. Conclusion 
In this work, we proposed a DQN based OSL algorithm. The proposed 
algorithm takes the matrices of robot trajectory, the measured airflow 
direction, the measured odor hit, and the current map as the input layer 
of a convolutional neural network. The convolutional neural network 
outputs the value of each possible action. The olfactory robot can take 
the action with the highest value to find the odor source. 
The proposed algorithm is evaluated under four map settings. For 
each setting, different odor source release rates are set. The proposed 
algorithm yield a good success rate and distance overhead when the 
olfactory robot starts from the downwind direction of the odor source, 
and slightly lower results when the robot starts from the upwind di -
rection. The proposed algorithm is robust to different odor source 
release rate, except that when starting from the upwind direction and 
there is an obstacle between the robot and the odor source, the perfor -
mance will decrease if the odor source release rate is low. 
Overall, the proposed algorithm achieves better performance than a 
traditional gradient climbing algorithm and moth-inspired algorithm, 
especially in complex indoor scenarios, relying on knowledge of the map 
of the scenario and the position of the robot, which the above mentioned 
traditional algorithms do not require. 
Future work will be focused on promoting the algorithm ’ s compu -
tational efficiency and reduce the information it requires. More 
advanced reinforcement learning algorithms and source localization 
algorithms dedicated for larger maps (e.g. undersea or outdoor sce -
narios) and more complex scenarios (e.g. higher dimensions, more ob -
stacles) need to be further studied. We will also try to improve the 
algorithm by centering the map around the robot as [56] did. Perfor -
mance comparison with more elaborated OSL methods and experiments 
on real robots will be conducted.

We implement this algorithm in our simulation. The olfactory robot is programmed to be able to move in four directions, the same as the proposed 
DQN based algorithm. It is assumed that the robot has active sniffing, and can measure odor hits on the four nearest grid points from its location at

Appendix C. Surge-Cast algorithm 
Surge-Cast algorithm [38] is a widely studied bio-inspired algorithm. It combines upwind surge and cross-wind casting. When in the plume, the 
robot moves straight upwind until it loses the plume. Then it moves cross-wind to reacquire the plume. This process is illustrated in Fig. C.18 . 
When the Surge-Cast algorithm is implemented in our simulation, the hit threshold we set to define the olfactory robot is “ in plume ” or “ out of 
plume ” is 10. the olfactory robot is programmed to do a cross-wind scan first to find the plume, then move upwind while measuring odor hits at every 
step until it loses the plume. It will then move in the crosswind line back and forth until it gets inside the plume. This iteration will keep running until it 
reaches the odor source. The robot is also programmed to be able to avoid boundaries and obstacles.
